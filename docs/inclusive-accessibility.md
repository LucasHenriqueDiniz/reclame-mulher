# Accessibility and inclusion for low literacy

Guidelines for making the platform usable by people with little digital experience, low literacy,
slow reading, little visual literacy, and a fear of getting things wrong.

The goal is not to satisfy technical accessibility. It is to remove barriers people actually hit:

- understanding a screen without effort
- finishing a task without reading long text
- feeling safe enough to continue
- asking for help without embarrassment
- managing on a phone, in a hurry, in the street, on a bad connection

Every quoted string below is product copy and stays in Portuguese: it is what a Brazilian user
reads, so translating it would make this document wrong about the app.

## Who this is for

The platform has to work for very different people:

- people who have never used a system like this
- people who read little, or with difficulty
- users who depend almost entirely on a phone
- older people
- people in a hurry, anxious, or with little patience for filling in a registration
- people with little formal schooling
- people who need someone else's help to enter their data
- people with partial or total visual impairment

## Design principles

### 1. One action per screen

Each screen should answer one simple question: who you are, what you want to do, or what the next
step is. Avoid screens with several paths at once; if there is more than one important decision,
split it into steps.

### 2. Less text, more clarity

Short text works better than beautiful text.

Prefer short sentences, concrete words, direct verbs and real examples. Avoid legal jargon, product
terms like "onboarding" or "fluxo", long sentences with several clauses, and big paragraphs on a
registration screen.

### 3. Always show context

If someone is in the middle of a flow, they need to know where they are, what is left, what happens
next, and how to go back.

### 4. A visible action

The primary button has to be obvious: consistent colour, generous size, a direct label, no
ambiguity.

### 5. Less fear

Plenty of people abandon a system because they think they will get it wrong. So: say that they can
go back, show autosave where it exists, keep messages calm, and avoid aggressive or technical
errors.

## Language guidelines

### Reading level

Write for basic reading. One idea per sentence. Avoid the passive voice, long paragraphs, internal
abbreviations, and acronyms with no explanation.

### Rewriting examples

Bad:

- "Preencha seus dados para concluir o onboarding"

Better:

- "Vamos montar sua conta em poucos passos"

Bad:

- "Autorizacao insuficiente para executar a operacao"

Better:

- "Voce nao tem permissão para fazer isso"

Bad:

- "Campo invalido"

Better:

- "Digite um email valido"

### Tone

Respectful, simple, direct and welcoming — without talking down to anyone.

## Priority flows

### Getting in

The ideal path is: choose a profile, create an account, confirm access, sign in.

Make the login and registration buttons very clear, do not hide the difference between a person and
a company, and let people move forward without reading much.

### Registration

Registration has to be as small as possible: ask only for what is needed, put examples under the
fields, split it into steps, show visual progress, and allow going back without losing data.

A suggested split: step 1, who you are; step 2, basic data; step 3, confirm and continue.

### Filing a complaint

The flow needs guiding: choose the company, describe the problem, attach evidence if there is any,
review, send.

Where possible, let someone save and come back later, and explain in plain language what happens
after sending.

### Following up

After sending, a person needs to understand whether the complaint arrived, whether the company
replied, what is still missing, and when to come back. Simple statuses, consistent colours, a
timeline and short messages do that.

## Interface components

### Primary button

Generous height, short text, high contrast, a clear action label.

Good labels:

- "Continuar"
- "Enviar reclamacao"
- "Entrar"
- "Ver resposta"
- "Salvar e continuar"

Avoid vague ones:

- "Prosseguir"
- "Confirmar"
- "Avancar"
- "Submeter"

### Secondary button

For going back, cancelling, editing, or finding out more.

### Choice cards

Choice cards matter for a low-literacy audience because they reduce reading. Use a large icon, a
very short title, a description of one line and at most two, and make the action clear at the end.

Good:

- "Sou uma pessoa"
- "Sou uma empresa"

Not:

- "Pessoa fisica"
- "Entidade corporativa"
- "Perfil de uso da plataforma"

### Form fields

Label always visible. A placeholder is an example, not the only explanation. Generous height for
touch. The error right below the field, and one error at a time where possible.

Show an example — "Ex: maria@email.com" — say when a field is optional, and avoid complicated masks
that nothing requires.

### Error messages

A good message points at the problem, says how to fix it, and uses plain language:

- "Digite seu email"
- "A senha precisa ter 6 caracteres ou mais"
- "Esse CPF ja foi usado"

A bad one:

- "Erro ao processar sua requisicao"
- "Validation failed"
- "Unexpected server response"

## Visual accessibility

### Contrast

Strong contrast between text and background, button and background, and an error and the rest of
the interface.

### Typography

A legible face, generous sizes, no very light weights, and enough line spacing.

### Hierarchy

Each screen in a clear order: logo or title, the point of the screen, the primary action, then help
or alternatives.

### Touch target size

Big enough for large fingers, one-handed use, and a small phone.

### Focus state

Every interactive element shows a visible focus: links, buttons, inputs, menus.

### Never colour alone

Error, success and warning states carry a colour, an icon, and explanatory text.

## Accessibility for low reading

### Alternatives to text

For people who do not read well, the interface has to speak by other means: icons, a consistent
layout, examples, audio, and a human.

### Guided reading

Offer the option to hear the screen, hear the step explained, and hear the form's instructions —
most useful at login, registration, the start of a complaint, and the send confirmation.

### Useful repetition

To reduce mistakes: repeat the context where it helps, show the selected company's name, show what
has been filled in, and show what is left.

## Help and support

### A human

For this audience, human help is not an extra. It is part of the product. A "Preciso de ajuda"
button, a fast support channel, the option to talk to someone on WhatsApp, and simple standard
answers.

### Contextual help

Help at the moment someone gets stuck: a hint beside the field, an "O que e isso?" button, a small
explanation right under the title.

### Help without embarrassment

The copy has to make clear that asking is normal:

- "Se quiser, voce pode pedir ajuda para continuar"

## Product suggestions

### Simple mode

Little text, big buttons, visual examples, even shorter language. It could be the default on
mobile.

### Audio mode

Hear the screen title, hear it field by field, hear the help messages, hear the final summary.

### Assisted flow

An experience in steps: one decision at a time, strong visual confirmation, a help button always
visible.

### Autosave

Saving automatically prevents abandonment — above all for long complaints, registration with
documents, and company forms.

### Easy recovery

If someone leaves halfway: say the progress was saved, and let them continue where they stopped.

## Screen by screen

### Home

Explain what the platform does without demanding long reading: a short hero, three main benefits,
a clear button to start.

### Login

Get in fast: less text, an email field with an example, a clear password field, a direct error, a
strong button.

### Registration

Choose a profile without confusion: very visual cards, little text, a very clear action.

### Onboarding

Finish registration without overload: split into steps, show progress, save a draft.

### Filing a complaint

Record a problem with as little friction as possible: plain language, an example in each field,
optional attachments with help, a final review before sending.

### Blog

Inform with easy reading: short titles, visual emphasis, cards with a small summary.

## What success looks like

The platform is better for this audience when:

- a person works out the next step without asking
- registration fits in a few screens
- the primary buttons are obvious
- errors say exactly what to do
- the interface still makes sense without fluent reading
- someone can finish a task on a phone without frustration

## Suggested order

**Short term.** Simplify the copy on login, registration and onboarding; add examples in inputs;
make help visible; improve the error states; make the buttons big and consistent.

**Medium term.** Simple mode, audio support, autosave, a guided complaint flow.

**Long term.** A full review of the platform's language, testing with real low-literacy users, and
iterating on what watching them shows.

## Testing with real users

Test with simple tasks: sign in, choose a profile, file a complaint, go back to edit something,
find the right company.

Watch where the person hesitates, what they ignore, which part they read out loud, where they ask
for help, and which screen they give up on.

## Conclusion

If the platform means to serve people who do not command technology or reading, the rule is:

**less interface, more guidance**

Which means less text, split steps, obvious choices, and assuming the person may be afraid of
getting it wrong.
